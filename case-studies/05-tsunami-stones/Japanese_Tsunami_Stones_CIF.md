# Case Study #5 — Japanese Tsunami Stones

### Axiom 5 Warning Transmission with Measurable Compliance Outcomes

*CIF Ancient Systems Test Series · Case Study #5*
Author: Derek Hone · Remnant Fieldworks Inc.

---

## Status

This is a CIF design analysis of a **well-documented and comparatively uncontested** case. The existence of Japan's tsunami stones (*tsunami-ishi*), their inscriptions, and the survival of the village of Aneyoshi in the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami are supported by contemporaneous reporting (including *The New York Times*, 2011) and subsequent documentation. The one point requiring discipline is causal: it is established that Aneyoshi's settlement sat above the 2011 inundation line and that a warning stone instructs building above that point; the *strength of the causal link* between the stone specifically and the survival outcome is highly plausible but should be stated as inference, not proof, because settlement elevation was also reinforced by lived memory of the 1896 and 1933 disasters. This case is the purest test in the series of CIF Axiom 5: *safe operation must be bounded, and warnings must remain legible across generations.*

---

## 1. Core Question

Giza (Case Study #1) exposed the hardest unsolved problem in deep-time transmission: how do you make a **warning** that stays a warning after language, institutions, and context are lost? Nuclear-waste marker programs wrestle with the same problem across 10,000-year horizons and have no proven solution.

Japan's tsunami stones are a rare natural experiment that tests the problem on a shorter but *real* timescale, with an actual life-or-death outcome:

> Can a passive, low-technology warning encode a behavioral instruction ("do not build below this line") legibly and persuasively enough that a community obeys it generations after the disaster that motivated it — and does obedience measurably change survival?

Unlike most of the series, this case has a **measurable compliance outcome**: the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami stress-tested every stone along the Sanriku coast simultaneously, and the results are on the record.

---

## 2. Evidence Discipline

Three claims must be separated:

- **The stones exist and carry warning content.** Established. Over 600 tsunami stones stand along the Japanese coast; some are centuries old, with a concentration erected after the 1896 Meiji-Sanriku (~22,000 deaths) and 1933 Shōwa-Sanriku disasters.
- **Aneyoshi survived 2011 and its settlement sat above the inundation line.** Established through contemporaneous reporting. The village's 11 households were unscathed; the water reportedly stopped short of the marker.
- **The stone *caused* the survival.** Plausible inference, not proof. Aneyoshi relocated to high ground after 1933 (when the village was reduced to four survivors), and that relocation — reinforced by the stone, by schooling, and by living family memory — is what placed homes above the 2011 line. The stone is best understood as one durable node in a transmission system, not a sole cause.

A second discipline point: tsunami stones are frequently cited as uniformly effective. They were not. The documented pattern is *mixed compliance* — many communities ignored or forgot their stones as coastlines modernized and faith shifted to engineered seawalls. Aneyoshi is celebrated precisely because it is an exception. An honest CIF analysis must treat the failures as data equal in weight to the success.

---

## 3. Three Evidence Classes

### ESTABLISHED

- Japan's Sanriku coast has a long, well-documented history of catastrophic tsunamis (notably 1896, 1933, 1960 Chile-origin, and 2011 Tōhoku).
- Hundreds of tsunami stones exist; some carry explicit behavioral instructions, others are memorials or mass-grave markers.
- The Aneyoshi stone (erected 1933) carries an instruction to the effect of: "High dwellings are the peace and harmony of our descendants. Remember the calamity of the great tsunamis. Do not build any homes below this point."
- The 2011 Tōhoku tsunami caused on the order of ~16,000–18,000 deaths and Aneyoshi's households survived intact.
- Many other coastal communities that had expanded below historical inundation lines suffered heavy losses in 2011.

### PLAUSIBLE INFERENCE

- The stone materially contributed to Aneyoshi's decision to keep settlement above the line across generations.
- Warnings phrased as **specific, spatial, actionable instructions** ("build above this point") transmit behavior more reliably than abstract memorials.
- Reinforcement channels (local schooling, family memory, repeated disasters) were necessary complements to the stone; the stone alone, without a living interpretive layer, would have been weaker.
- The commonly cited "memory fades after three generations" (attributed to Prof. Fumihiko Imamura, Tohoku University) captures a real decay pattern in warning compliance.

### SPECULATIVE

- That the stone alone, absent all reinforcement, would have secured compliance.
- Precise counterfactual death tolls attributable specifically to stones ignored versus heeded.
- That the Aneyoshi model can be transplanted wholesale to other cultures or to 10,000-year horizons.

## 4. CIF Axiom Analysis

**Axiom 1 — Systems operate within structured fields.** The warning operates inside coupled fields: the physical field (coastline, elevation, wave dynamics), the social field (village governance, schooling, family), and the economic field (pressure to live near the sea for fishing and commerce). The stone's success or failure in any given village is determined less by the stone than by which field dominates — safety memory or economic pull. Aneyoshi is where the safety field held.

**Axiom 2 — Productive transfer depends on appropriate matching.** The Aneyoshi stone matches its medium to its message with unusual precision: it is *sited at the exact elevation* it references, so the instruction ("do not build below this point") is self-locating. The warning is not an abstract fact to be recalled; it is a line on the actual hillside. This is a near-optimal match of message to receiver and to place — arguably better matched than Giza's ratios, because no decoding step is required.

**Axiom 3 — Drift and noise may contain recoverable inheritance.** The stones themselves are a civilization's recirculated *diagnostic waste*: each was erected from the lesson of a specific catastrophe. This is CIF Axiom 3 in its most literal social form — a disaster (waste/failure) converted into an inherited control. The failure mode is drift of *attention*, not drift of the artifact: the stones did not degrade physically nearly as fast as the population's willingness to heed them.

**Axiom 4 — Coherence is the condition of stable operation.** Aneyoshi maintained coherence between *knowledge* and *behavior* across ~78 years and multiple generations — the knowledge stayed coupled to action. The widespread failures elsewhere are coherence breakdowns of a specific kind: the physical warning persisted while the behavioral coupling drifted. This decouples two things Giza treated together: an artifact can retain full physical and semantic coherence and still fail, because **coherence of understanding is not coherence of compliance.**

**Axiom 5 — Safe operation must be bounded.** This is the case's center. The stone *is* a hard boundary: a literal line below which building is forbidden. It encodes a non-harm constraint in the most durable, lowest-dependency medium available. And it exposes the deepest lesson: a boundary is only as good as the receiving system's willingness to honor it. The stones prove that **encoding a boundary durably is the solved half of the problem; enforcing it across generations against economic pressure is the unsolved half.**

**Axiom 6 — Trunks generate forests.** The tsunami stone is a reusable trunk — a pattern of "site a durable, specific, spatial warning at the hazard boundary" — that generated a forest of 600+ instances along the coast. It also sits in the same trunk lineage as modern deep-time warning efforts (nuclear-waste markers), which inherit the same unsolved enforcement problem at vastly longer horizons.

## 5. CIF Pillar Analysis

**Pillar 1 — Sense the field.** The stones encode precise observation of the hazard: real inundation lines from real events. The knowledge is empirical, not mythologized — the line is where the water actually reached.

**Pillar 2 — Match the response.** The response is matched to the hazard and to human behavior: a permanent, unpowered, unambiguous, spatially self-locating instruction. It requires no institution to remain physically present — the Giza durability lesson — but adds the specificity Giza lacked.

**Pillar 3 — Adapt under change.** Stone cannot adapt, and here that is mostly a virtue: the inundation line does not change on human timescales, so a fixed instruction stays valid. The failure was not in the stone's inability to adapt but in the *population* adapting in the wrong direction — modernizing toward the coast and trusting seawalls over stones.

**Pillar 4 — Recirculate recoverable waste.** Each stone recirculates the lesson of a past disaster into future protection — a working incident-to-control loop. Post-2011, this loop ran again: renewed interest in the stones, and proposals to preserve ruined buildings as physical warnings (echoing the Hiroshima Peace Memorial), recirculating the 2011 failure into new controls.

**Pillar 5 — Enforce non-harm coherence.** The stones are pure non-harm devices. But 2011 demonstrated the limit of *encoding without enforcement*: where the boundary was honored (Aneyoshi), it held; where attention had drifted, the identical class of warning failed. The pillar's requirement — that warnings remain legible and honored across generations — was met in the exception and failed in the rule.

## 6. Fidelity Assessment

Qualitative status by inheritance layer:

- **Physical form:** Substantially preserved. The stones are durable, low-maintenance, and remained physically present and readable across the full interval.
- **Data:** Substantially preserved. The specific spatial instruction (build above this line) and the memorialized event survived intact.
- **Method:** Preserved and self-evident. Unlike Giza, no method must be reconstructed — the instruction is direct and requires no decoding key.
- **Intent:** Preserved. The intent (protect descendants) is stated explicitly in the inscription. This is a major advance over Giza, whose intent is disputed.
- **Semantic meaning:** Preserved where the interpretive layer (schooling, family, ceremony) persisted; degraded where it lapsed. Meaning survival tracked the living layer, not the stone.
- **Error log:** Partially present at the system scale. The very existence of newer stones records earlier disasters, and the mixed 2011 outcomes are now a documented error log for the *warning system as a whole* — arguably the richest error log in the series, because we can see exactly which warnings were heeded and which were not, and what happened.

---

## 7. Implications for the Present

First and central: **encoding a warning durably is the easy half; sustaining compliance is the hard half.** The tsunami stones solved substrate, legibility, specificity, and self-location — every technical problem Giza left open — and *still* failed in most communities, because behavioral coupling decayed. For nuclear-waste markers, long-term facility siting, and any modern deep-time warning, this is the decisive lesson: budget for compliance decay, not just marker durability.

Second: **specific, spatial, actionable warnings outperform abstract ones.** "Do not build below this point," sited at that point, beat generic memorials. Modern risk communication (flood maps, evacuation routes, building codes) should inherit this: bind the warning to the exact place and action, minimize the interpretation step.

Third: **the ~three-generation decay pattern is a design constant to engineer against.** Warning systems must include active refresh mechanisms — schooling, ritual, drills, re-inscription — because passive persistence of the artifact does not prevent decay of attention. Aneyoshi succeeded because it *taught the stone*, not merely kept it.

Fourth: modern engineered defenses can *displace* inherited warnings. Faith in seawalls led communities to build below historical lines; when the walls were overtopped in 2011, the displaced traditional boundary would have saved them. New capability that quietly retires an old, cheaper, more robust control is a recognizable CIF drift pattern with direct modern analogs (automation replacing human judgment, redundancy removed because "the new system is better").

---

## 8. Design Requirements / Lessons Derived

1. **Bind the warning to the hazard boundary itself.** Site the instruction at the exact line it describes so it is self-locating and needs no external key.
2. **Make warnings specific and actionable, not commemorative.** Encode the *behavior* required, not just the memory of loss.
3. **Pair the passive marker with an active refresh loop.** Schooling, ritual, and drills must re-transmit the warning every generation to counter the three-generation decay.
4. **Do not let new capability silently retire an old control.** Adding seawalls should not delete the "build above the line" boundary; layered defenses beat substituted ones.
5. **Treat compliance, not encoding, as the primary risk.** Design and budget for behavioral coupling decay as the dominant failure mode.
6. **Preserve the mixed outcomes as the error log.** The heeded-versus-ignored record is the most valuable inheritance; keep it legible so future generations see the cost of forgetting.

---

## 9. Conclusions

1. Tsunami stones are the purest test in the series of CIF Axiom 5: a durable, bounded, non-harm warning with a real, measurable compliance outcome.
2. They solved every *technical* deep-time warning problem Giza left open — durability, legibility, specificity, explicit intent, self-location — and required no decoding key.
3. Despite this, the system's outcome in 2011 was *mixed*: Aneyoshi survived; many communities that had let attention drift did not. Encoding was solved; enforcement was not.
4. The success case (Aneyoshi) worked because a living interpretive layer — schooling and family memory — actively re-transmitted the stone's message across generations.
5. The failures were coherence breakdowns between knowledge and behavior, accelerated by economic pull and misplaced faith in engineered defenses.
6. The decisive modern lesson is that warning durability is necessary but not sufficient; compliance must be actively sustained against a roughly three-generation decay constant.
7. The case gives the series its richest error log: a continent-scale, single-event stress test showing exactly which warnings held and which failed.

## Falsification Check

**What finding would contradict CIF?** CIF (Axiom 5, Pillar 5) predicts that a durable, well-matched, specific warning, coupled to an active refresh layer, should produce better survival outcomes than warnings that are equally durable but decoupled from a living interpretive layer. CIF would be challenged if:

- Communities with **strong, well-maintained** warning traditions had fared *no better* in 2011 than communities that had entirely forgotten their stones — i.e., if compliance made no survival difference.
- Or if the survival of Aneyoshi were shown to be pure elevation luck, wholly unrelated to any transmitted warning or behavior (e.g., if equally low-lying nearby villages that *ignored* their stones had survived identically).

**Does the evidence approach falsification?** No — and this is the series' cleanest *confirmation*, which is worth stating as plainly as the counterexamples. The pattern runs the way CIF predicts: where the warning was actively sustained, behavior stayed coupled to knowledge and the outcome was survival; where the interpretive layer decayed, the identical durable warning failed. The one honest caveat is causal attribution — the stone did not act alone, and elevation plus lived memory plus schooling are entangled. CIF's claim is not "the stone saved them" but "a bounded warning survives *as a control* only when an active layer keeps it coupled to behavior," and 2011 supports exactly that. This case is counted as **supporting** CIF, with the causal-attribution caveat explicit. Notably, it supports CIF while *simultaneously* delivering CIF's most uncomfortable lesson: most of the warnings failed.

---

## Final CIF Verdict

The tsunami stones answer the question Giza could only pose. Giza asked how a warning could stay a warning after context is lost; the Sanriku coast shows that the technical half of that problem is solvable — you can carve a specific, self-locating, intent-stated boundary into a medium that outlasts institutions — and that the *human* half is the one that kills people. The stones did not fail because they eroded or because their meaning became illegible. They failed, where they failed, because a later generation read them, understood them perfectly, and built below the line anyway. **A boundary encoded is not a boundary enforced.** That is the whole of Axiom 5, written in stone along a coastline, and paid for in 2011.
